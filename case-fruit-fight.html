Cases/Fruit Fight

A card game where real cards come alive on your phone

Fruit Fight is a new Israeli card game: 52 creatures, each a cross between an animal and a fruit. You scan a printed card, it unlocks in the app, and you train it and send it into a real-time battle. I designed the whole app on my own: every screen, the visual system and the store listing.

01

Overview

Fruit Fight (in Hebrew, הפריצורים) is a card game that lives in two worlds. There are 52 creatures, each a cross between an animal and a fruit, printed on 208 collectible cards sold in shops across Israel. Scan a card with your phone and the creature comes alive in the app. There you train it from level 1 to 100 and send it into a real-time battle against a bot or a friend.

I was the only designer on the app. I designed every screen and flow, the visual system and the store listing, working directly with the product owner and the developer who built it.

02

The challenge

The players are young, and most of them have never seen a game like this. In the first minute they need to understand three things without reading a manual:

  • Real cards unlock creatures. You scan a physical card, and it becomes a creature on your phone.
  • Creatures get stronger. Training raises attack and defence, and each creature's ability grows tier by tier.
  • Battles are simple and fast. Drag a card onto one of three lanes and break the enemy castle.

All of this had to work in Hebrew, right to left, with an English version mirrored from it. It also had to follow strict store rules: the game is fully playable without cards, and nothing may suggest you have to buy cards to play.

03

My role

  • The whole app. Home, battle hub, team picker, VS intro, battle arena, results, powers guide, creature pop-up, achievements, the "My Cards" album, the training camp and each creature's level ladder.
  • Visual system. Dark panels, yellow accents, a colour for each creature group (attackers, defenders, balanced, wizard), purple level badges, and greyscale for anything you don't have yet.
  • Scanning and collecting. I designed the album like a real sticker album: every one of the 208 cards has a fixed place. Missing cards stay in black and white, so you can see exactly what's left to find.
  • Store listing. App Store and Google Play screenshots in Hebrew and English.
  • Hand-off. Figma frames with every state, in RTL, ready for the developer.
04

Process

Three iterations of one question: how do you hold the phone?

  1. HorizontalEverything in landscape. Great for battle, awkward for menus and the album.
  2. VerticalEverything in portrait. Easy one-handed, but the three lanes no longer fit.
  3. MixedMenus and cards in portrait, battle in landscape, with a friendly "rotate your phone" screen in between.

The final version gives each task the orientation that suits it. Collecting and training happen with one hand. The battle turns sideways so all three lanes and both castles are on screen at once.

05

Solution

The whole game in six steps, the way a new player meets it: scan, build a team, get to know your creatures, train them and fight. Every screen shows rather than tells: big creature art, a few words and one clear action.

These six images are also the store screenshots I designed for the App Store and Google Play, in English and Hebrew.

07

What I learned

Show, don't explain. When players don't read long text, the picture has to carry the rule: colour means yours, black and white means still to find.

Orientation is a design decision. It took three versions to see that the answer wasn't "landscape" or "portrait" but the right one for each task.

Designing in a language I don't read. Working in Hebrew RTL taught me to build layouts that mirror cleanly and to check every text with native speakers before release.
