Cases/Fruit Fight

Designing interaction for a physical mobile experience

Fruit Fight connects printed collectible cards with a mobile game. It is a case about how the interface changes when the way you hold and move the phone becomes part of the game.

01

Context

Fruit Fight (in Hebrew, הפריצורים) is a card game that lives in two worlds. Printed collectible cards are sold in shops across Israel; scan one with your phone and its creature comes alive in the app, where you train it and send it into a real-time battle.

I was the only designer on the app and owned the whole product design, working directly with the product owner and the developer. It took 9 months and several iterations to get from the first screen to release on the App Store.

02

The challenge

The players are children, and most of them have never seen a game like this. In the first minute they had to understand the game without reading a manual: real cards unlock creatures, training makes them stronger, and battles are fast.

Constraints
  • Language. Hebrew, right to left, with an English version mirrored from it.
  • Store rules. The game must be fully playable without cards, and nothing may suggest you have to buy them.
  • The phone itself. Collecting is a one-handed task; a battle on three lanes needs the whole screen.
03

How do you hold the phone?

The interaction model had to work with the way players physically hold and move the device, not just with the visual layout of the screen. I tested three answers at the design stage:

  1. HorizontalEverything in landscape. Great for battle, awkward for menus and the album.
  2. VerticalEverything in portrait. Easy one-handed, but the three lanes no longer fit.
  3. MixedMenus and cards in portrait, battle in landscape, with a friendly “rotate your phone” screen in between.

I chose the mixed model. The trade-off is one extra moment, turning the phone before a battle; in return, each task gets the orientation that suits it. Deciding this before development avoided rework later.

Rules shown, not explained

Instead of instructions, I gave every rule a visual: one colour per creature group, purple level badges, and black and white for cards you don't have yet. One clear action per screen, big art, few words.

Solution

05

Outcome

  • Validated with children. In playtests, children understood how to play without explanations.
  • Shipped. The app is live on the App Store in Israel, 9 months after the first screen; the Android version is on its way.
  • Two languages from one system. Hebrew RTL with a mirrored English version, so the developer worked from one set of layouts.
06

What I learned

Orientation is a design decision. The answer wasn't “landscape” or “portrait” but the right one for each task.

Designing in a language I don't read taught me to build layouts that mirror cleanly and to check every text with native speakers before release.
